# Sources

Post: 2026-11-13-1433-how-fast-is-your-website (Reel, 4 scenes, 16.4 seconds). Both pages were opened on 13 Nov 2026 in this run, each after a web search returned its URL.

## 1. Main content on screen within 2.5 seconds is what Google calls good
- Used on: scene 2 ("Good. Main content on screen by 2.5 seconds. That's Google's line."), the dial (the navy band on its rim runs from 0 to 2.5), caption ("If the main part of a page is on screen within 2.5 seconds, it calls that good.").
- Source: web.dev (Google), "Largest Contentful Paint (LCP)", by Philip Walton and Barry Pollard. https://web.dev/articles/lcp
- As the source states it: "LCP reports the render time of the largest image, text block, or video visible in the viewport, relative to when the user first navigated to the page." The page says LCP marks the point "when the page's main content has likely loaded". "To provide a good user experience, sites should strive to have Largest Contentful Paint of 2.5 seconds or less."
- Date: published 8 August 2019, last updated 4 September 2025. Checked 13 Nov 2026.
- Scope: Google's guidance for any website, worldwide. Not a statistic. "Main content" is our plain wording for the largest image or block of text on the first screen.

## 2. Past 4 seconds is what Google calls poor
- Used on: scene 3 ("Poor. Anything past 4 seconds. Google again."), the dial (the red band on its rim runs from 4 to 5), caption ("Past 4 seconds, it calls it poor.").
- Source A: the same web.dev page. The threshold diagram's description reads: "Good LCP values are 2.5 seconds or less, poor values are greater than 4.0 seconds, and anything in between needs improvement".
- Source B: Google for Developers, "About PageSpeed Insights". https://developers.google.com/speed/docs/insights/v5/about. Its thresholds table gives LCP as Good "[0, 2500ms]", Needs Improvement "(2500ms, 4000ms]", Poor "over 4000ms".
- Date: source A last updated 4 September 2025. Source B last updated 21 October 2024 (just over 24 months old, so it is used only as a second confirmation of the figure in source A). Checked 13 Nov 2026.

## 3. Google wants the good time to hold for most visitors, on phones as well as desktops
- Used on: caption only ("And it wants the good time to hold for most of your visitors, on phones as well as desktops.").
- Source: the same web.dev page, under "What is a good LCP score?".
- As the source states it: "To ensure you're hitting this target for most of your users, a good threshold to measure is the 75th percentile of page loads, segmented across mobile and desktop devices."
- Checked 13 Nov 2026.

## 4. pagespeed.web.dev is Google's own test, and you type in a web address
- Used on: scene 4 ("Time yours." and the address pagespeed.web.dev), caption ("Go to pagespeed.web.dev, which is Google's own test, and type in your web address.").
- Source A: the same web.dev page, "How to measure LCP", lists PageSpeed Insights under both its field tools and its lab tools and links it to https://pagespeed.web.dev/.
- Source B: Google for Developers, "About PageSpeed Insights": "PageSpeed Insights (PSI) reports on the user experience of a page on both mobile and desktop devices, and provides suggestions on how that page may be improved." It also says "PSI uses Lighthouse to analyze the given URL".
- Checked 13 Nov 2026.

## 5. The stopwatch
- The stopwatch is a drawing made for this post: one turn of the dial is five seconds, with a navy band on its rim to 2.5, a blue-grey band to 4 and a red band after it. It is not a real product and no brand of stopwatch is shown. The bands are the thresholds in claims 1 and 2.

## Not claimed
- No statistics, no named businesses, no clients and no results. We do not say how long the test takes, that it is free, what makes a site slow, or that speed changes where a site ranks. "How fast is your website?" and "Time yours." are prompts to check, not claims.
